EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Tidally influenced nearshore and estuary habitats can provide critical rearing and migration habitats for 
juvenile salmon, and their conservation and restoration has been identified as a priority for the recovery 
of Endangered Species Act (ESA) listed salmon in the Puget Sound (Beamer et al. 2005; Beamer et al. 
2018). However, the ability to identify restoration opportunities and track progress towards recovery 
(restoration) on a regional scale has been limited, as many existing datasets describing tidal restriction 
features (e.g., dikes and levees, roads and railways, shoreline armor, docks and piers, and water crossing 
features) are incomplete (i.e., mapping does not cover the entire region or is specific to one area, only 
certain feature types are included like roads, or data set does not include a complete spatial inventory 
and is missing features), outdated (i.e., datasets were developed in different years and do not represent 
the same time period or current conditions), fragmented across organizations (i.e., different jurisdictions 
like diking districts maintaining their own separate datasets), inconsistent methods or attributes (e.g., 
some datasets include detailed elevation and physical data, information on materials used for culverts or 
dikes, and some datasets focus on specific feature types like roads or just water crossing features). 
Previous mapping projects have identified significant data gaps in the completeness of regional datasets 
mapping nearshore tidal restriction features and tidally influenced habitats. Significant progress was 
made in developing a consistent spatial database of tidal restriction and tidally influenced habitats for 
Puget Sound’s large river deltas (Hall et al. 2021; WRCO 2019a; ESRP Project #18-2250). However, 
there was still a need to synthesize and standardize regional datasets for the rest of the Puget Sound 
nearshore. This project (WRCO 2019b; ESRP Project #20-1941P) addressed this data gap by extending 
the previously completed mapping of large river delta tidal restriction features to the rest of the Puget 
Sound, thereby developing consistent and updated regional mapping data for tidal restrictions (e.g., 
levees, dikes, floodgates, culverts, etc.) and tidally influenced habitat extents for Puget Sound nearshore 
habitats. 
We compiled data from 49 local, state, and federal datasets, including 267 vector layers mapping 
infrastructure, water crossings, hydrology, nearshore wetland and estuary habitat, land cover, and 
elevation data. We reviewed and reconciled these data with aerial imagery to create a consistent, up to 
date, and complete spatial database of Puget Sound nearshore tidal restrictions mapped as polylines and 
tidal influenced habitat mapped as polygons. Tidal restrictions features were classified by primary 
feature type (e.g., roads, railways, dikes/levees, armor) and water crossing feature types (e.g., bridges,

culverts, floodgates, docks/piers) and crossing types (e.g., ocean, streams/rivers, no visible channel). 
Restrictions were further classified by their impact on tidal connectivity (e.g., unrestricted, partially 
restricted, significantly restricted, and completely restricted tidal connectivity) based on their feature 
type and status (e.g., present or removed). We used the 2022 draft Pacific and Marine Estuarine 
Partnership Potential Wetland Extents to delineate areas within potential tidal influence based on 
elevation (PMEP 2022) and used our mapped tidal restriction network to segment and assign 
connectivity classifications to the potential tidally influenced habitat extent. We initially attempted to 
classify potential tidally influenced embayment and lagoonal habitat types into functional or geomorphic 
classifications (e.g., barrier lagoons, closed lagoons, etc.). However, it was determined (through regional 
review) that additional coordination is needed to develop a consistent classification schema to support 
regional salmon recovery planning and evaluation. Therefore, we focused on classification of potential 
tidally influenced habitat extents based on tidal connectivity ratings in this phase of the project.   
As a result of this project, we have mapped 3,149 kilometers of tidal restriction features, including 363 
kilometers of water crossing features, and 174,150 hectares of tidally influenced habitat using regional 
data and remote sensing for the Puget Sound nearshore. This represents newly mapped areas outside of 
previously mapped large river deltas (ESRP Project #18-2250). Although the large river deltas mapped 
as part of our previous project (ESRP Project #18-2250) are not included in this report, they are 
summarized in Hall et al. (2021) and the datasets are presented together in a WDFW hosted ArcGIS web 
map. Among the tidal restriction features (polylines) mapped as part of this project, we mapped and 
classified a total of: 2,705 kilometers of complete tidal restrictions, 12 kilometers of significant tidal 
restrictions, 334 kilometers of partial tidal restrictions, 28 kilometers of unrestricted crossings, 70 
kilometers of restrictions with unknown impacts, and 8,256 water crossing features (Figure 1). We note 
that among the water crossing features mapped, approximately 7.8% were not previously included in 
regional data sources and were identified using aerial imagery. We also mapped 293 kilometers of 
primary features (10% of the total length) that were not previously mapped in the regional data. This 
represents a significant improvement in the completeness of regional data describing tidal restriction 
features.

INTRODUCTION 
Puget Sound’s large river deltas and tidally influenced habitats associated with the rest of Puget Sound’s 
nearshore (e.g., lagoons, embayments, and other tidal wetland habitats) provide critical rearing, saltwater 
transition, and migration corridor habitats for juvenile salmon (Beamer et al. 2005; Beamer et al. 2018, 
Quinn 2018). Conservation and restoration of estuarine and tidally influenced habitats have been 
identified as top priorities for regional salmon recovery efforts in Puget Sound, especially for 
Endangered Species Act (ESA) listed Chinook Salmon populations, which depend on estuarine and 
nearshore habitats for juvenile rearing, refuge, and transition to marine life stages (Simenstad et al. 
1983; Simenstad and Cordell 2000; Beamer et al. 2005; Bottom et al. 2005a; Bottom et al. 2005b; Quinn 
2018). The area of functional (tidally connected and accessible) tidally influenced habitat is a regional 
salmon habitat indicator for large river deltas, pocket estuaries, and embayments in Puget Sound (see 
Puget Sound Partnership (PSP) Vital Sign and Salmon Habitat Indicators). Restoration of tidal 
connectivity or removal of features that block or restrict tidal processes represents a significant 
opportunity to increase functional and accessible tidally influenced habitat. However, the ability to 
identify restoration opportunities and track progress towards recovery (restoration) on a regional scale 
has been limited, as many existing tidal restriction datasets are incomplete (i.e., mapping does not cover 
the entire region or is specific to one area, only certain feature types are included like roads, or data set 
does not include a complete spatial inventory and is missing features), outdated (i.e., datasets were 
developed in different years and do not represent the same time period or current conditions), 
fragmented (i.e., different jurisdictions like diking districts maintaining their own separate datasets), or 
inconsistent (e.g., some datasets include detailed elevation and physical data, information on materials 
used for culverts or dikes, and some datasets focus on specific feature types like roads or just water 
crossing features). 
Many local, regional, state, and federal agency datasets have mapped tidal restriction, tidally influenced, 
or tidal wetland features and habitats (e.g., Simenstad et al. 2011; Beechie et al. 2017; PMEP 2018; 
Brophy et al. 2019; Confluence 2019; Ramirez 2019a; Stefankiv et al. 2019; WDFW 2020). However, 
these datasets differ in structure, extent, resolution, and the types of features mapped, and none of these 
existing datasets integrate tidal restriction features with associated tidally influenced habitats. 
Furthermore, regional datasets have been created for a variety of purposes and are therefore difficult to 
integrate, standardize, and use collectively at regional scales. A consistent database mapping tidal

restrictions, their type and connectivity impacts, and how these features influenced tidal connectivity to 
potential tidally influence habitats at a regional scale was a significant data gap for the Puget Sound 
region. This data gap limited our ability to develop regional-scale recovery planning data to support 
restoration planning and evaluation as well as evaluation of progress towards recovery, and we have 
directly addressed this data gap with completion of this project.  
For the purposes of this project, we defined tidal restrictions as human-made features (e.g., levees 
and dikes, roads and railways, and water crossing features like bridges, piers and docks, tide and 
floodgates, and culverts) that can disrupt, mute, or restrict tidal and floodplain functions, 
including flood storage and conveyance, nutrient processes, and hydrologic, sediment, wood, and 
biotic movement (adapted from Konrad 2015). The degree to which tidal processes are influenced 
varies among and within feature types depending on a number of physical factors (e.g., feature 
dimensions, elevation, landscape position, tidal and riverine processes). Regional recovery planning and 
evaluation programs (like those the Salmon Habitat Status and Trends Monitoring Program or the PSP 
Vital Signs and Salmon Habitat Indicators program) rely on delineation and characterization of current 
and potential tidally influenced habitats, including the degree to which tidal connectivity is restricted, at 
both regional and system scales. These data are needed to: (A) to develop meaningful recovery goals 
and targets; (B) identify, evaluate, and prioritize restoration opportunities; and (C) evaluate progress 
towards recovery goals and targets over time. This relies on consistent and accurate regional scale 
mapping that requires comprehensive identification of tidal connectivity restrictions.   
Significant progress was made in developing a consistent spatial database of tidal restriction and tidally 
influenced features through ESRP Project #18-2250 for Puget Sound’s large river deltas. For that 
project, we used a combination of regional data and remote sensing to synthesize and correct regional 
data and map missing features followed by regional review with local area experts and field validation 
surveys. We found that our approach was efficient in creating a consistent, standardized, and up to date 
spatial database of tidal restriction features for Puget Sound’s large river deltas while also identifying 
and mapping many features missing from available regional datasets (≈45% of the mapped features were 
not included in regional data). This project leveraged the already compiled regional datasets, 
standardized spatial database for the large river deltas, and remote sensing protocols to map tidal 
restriction features and tidally influenced extents for the rest of the Puget Sound shoreline. We worked 
with agencies, co-managers, and stakeholders to develop spatial data products that integrate with

existing habitat status and trends monitoring programs and metrics, address regional data gaps, and are 
readily accessible through online mapping applications to support restoration planning and evaluation of 
progress towards recovery and habitat restoration at a regional scale. This report serves as the final

and map tidal restriction and tidally influenced habitat extents, results and findings at both regional and 
Lead Entity (geographic areas that are used to report the status and trends of habitat in the Puget Sound 
region) reporting area scales, and appendices with supporting information on the methods and results.  
METHODS 
Study Area  
Previous mapping efforts (Hall et al. 2021) focused on delineating tidal restrictions within the Salmon 
Habitat Status and Trends Monitoring Program (SHSTMP) large river delta boundaries (NOAA 2016; 
Stefankiv et al. 2019) and tidally influenced extents in the Puget Sound Ecosystem Monitoring Program 
(PMEP) tidal exceedance polygons within the deltas (PMEP 2018; Brophy et al. 2019). This effort 
aimed to map tidal restrictions and tidally influenced extents for the remainder of the Puget Sound 
shoreline outside of the previously mapped large river deltas. While we compiled information at a 
regional scale, we also report summaries by Lead Entity (LE) areas, which are similar to Puget Sound 
sub-basins and counties but are grouped by stocks within a watershed. The LE areas are managed by a 
Lead Entity group for salmon recovery plans in Washington state. Out of the 25 LE areas in Washington 
state, we mapped within the 15 LE areas throughout the Puget Sound.  
To define the mapping extent with respect to the extent of tidal influence and proximity to the shoreline, 
in our previous mapping effort (Hall et al. 2021), we used the 2018 PMEP tidal exceedance dataset 
(Brophy et al. 2019) as the tidally influenced extent, and we first considered this dataset given that it 
provided coverage for the rest of the Puget Sound. However, there were inconsistencies in coverage for 
smaller tidally influenced embayments, lagoons, and other features. We worked with PMEP to address 
this issue, and they provided an updated layer in 2022 to improve coverage of these features and 
published a draft spatial dataset that we used for this mapping effort. We found that the updated draft 
2022 PMEP extent vastly improved the coverage of pocket estuaries and embayments identified in the 
PSP Common Indicators dataset (PSP 2019; Hall et al. 2019). However, some features were still missing 
or misaligned and we used the Pocket Estuaries point dataset (PSP 2019; Hall et al. 2019) to identify 
tidally influenced habitats not captured in the updated 2022 PMEP dataset, and used aerial imagery and

elevation data (DEMs) to delineate the extent of those habitats along the Puget Sound shoreline. To 
complement regional datasets and to capture features that could influence tidal connectivity or processes 
in tidally influenced habitats, we also mapped restrictions within four nearshore strata, 1) seaward to the 
Nearshore Geospatial Framework (NGF) shoreline which is based on the WA DNR ShoreZone 
shoreline, 2) within the Beach Strategies NGF 0-200 ft polygon, 3) within the PMEP extent or aerial 
imagery-mapped extent, and/or 4) within a 200-ft buffer of the PMEP or aerially imagery mapped tidal 
exceedance extent (Figure 4). It is important to note, that with the draft 2022 PMEP extent was used as 
our basis of mapping represents the modelled maximum extent of tidal flooding based on current 
topography and extreme tidal water levels, what we describe as potential tidally influenced habitat 
extent (completely restricted non-tidal), does not represent historical extent. The elevation models were 
derived from modern datasets and already integrate some degree of fill and development in the 
landscape (PMEP 2018; Brophy et al. 2019).

Regional data discovery and processing 
In our previous project (ESRP Project #18-2250), we compiled spatial datasets that contained habitat, 
infrastructure, and hydrography data for estuarine and delta habitats from online sources and direct 
requests to regional data stewards, with an emphasis on data that describe features that block or 
potentially mute tidal connectivity. These data were also considered in this effort, but we performed 
additional data queries to identify any sources that may have been published since or missed in our 
initial search. We developed an archive of all compiled regional data layers as part of this project, but a 
summary of the regional data layers compiled and consider in this study are provided in Table 1 and 
Appendix A: Data Discovery Log.

We used QGIS (QGIS 2024) to visualize and process all regional datasets. The fix geometry tool was 
used to address any geometry errors in the compiled datasets, and we used a unique ID using the dataset 
name and row number was given to every row of every dataset to track features for future data 
processing. This identifier can be used to link features included in the final database to the original data 
source, which may contain useful information that can be reviewed and accessed. Data were projected to 
a common coordinate reference system (CRS), WA State Plane South NAD 83 HARN (EPSG 2927) and 
saved as geopackage file (a QGIS layer package that allows for storage of large datasets of multiple 
geometries and data types) and geodatabase file (similar to a geopackage, this file type is for ESRI 
products). A data inventory was maintained with names and descriptions of all data discovered including 
the source, file type (e.g., GIS shapefile or geodatabase), spatial extent, data year, access date, 
permissions, and a summary of the data included. The data type (e.g., vector: line, point, or polygon, or 
raster) was also recorded.

Standardized spatial database 
Variations in data structure, accuracy, and extent limited the utility of available datasets at a regional 
scale, and this highlighted the need for a consistent regional database structure and remote-sensing based 
synthesis approach. As part of the ESRP Project #18-2250, we developed protocols that created a new 
data structure that streamlined information, geometry, and attribution for mapping tidal restrictions and 
tidally influenced extents in the Puget Sound large river deltas (WRCO 2019, Hall et al. 2021). For 
mapping tidal restrictions and tidally influenced extents along the Puget Sound nearshore, we utilized 
the same general data structure to ensure compatibility between the datasets but made key updates to 
capture characteristics specific to the shoreline and nearshore, which are described in this report (see 
also Appendix B: Mapping methods and data structure). We synthesized available regional data sources 
into our spatial database for both tidal restrictions and tidally influenced extent datasets, with attributes 
that described feature types (for tidal restrictions, primary and water crossing structure, tidal 
connectivity impacts, key physical information, and the spatial location of the feature (e.g., study extent 
strata, shore type, drift cell, and Lead Entity boundary). The database structures were also designed to 
capture information on the source of mapped features (e.g., regional data, remote sensing, regional 
review, or field validation), and to document corrections or updates identified using remote sensing, 
field validation, or regional review to features mapped in a regional dataset. In addition, we created 
attributes to flag features for field and/or local area expert review and validation, as well as certainty 
ratings for feature type and connectivity to support review, interpretation, and updating of the database. 
The full data structure and descriptions of attributes and values in the standardized database are 
described in more detail in Appendix B: Mapping methods and data structure. 
Tidal restrictions 
All tidal restriction features were digitized as polylines with a single line representing the approximate 
elevational top or middle of the tidal restriction feature. Digitizing included the following key steps: (1) 
synthesizing redundant features and correct spatial alignment, (2) updating removed or modified 
features, (3) digitizing missing features, (4) classifying feature types and certainty, and (5) classifying 
tidal connectivity and certainty. Features were classified using regional data and/or aerial imagery in a 
two-tiered nested structure that included a primary feature type and water crossing feature type (where 
applicable) as described in Table 2. The first tier included roads and railways, dikes and levees, armored 
or filled banks, footpaths, and boat ramps, which were classified as primary feature types. The second 
tier included water crossing structure type, if applicable. Water crossings were defined by the presence

of connectivity or flow under a solid feature as identified by aerial imagery or as mapped in the regional 
data. If for a given line segment, the primary feature was not also a water crossing, the water crossing 
type was reported as NULL (e.g., a section of road without culverts or other water crossing structures). 
If the water crossing was not part of a primary feature (e.g., agricultural bridges and culverts without a 
graded road), then the primary feature was classified as NULL. If a water crossing existed, the line 
feature was segmented into a separate feature (using the mapped regional data location and aerial 
imagery as reference) to the full extent of the crossing and the segment was classified by the primary 
type (e.g., roads, railways, dikes, levees, armored banks) and the water crossing feature (e.g., floodgates, 
self-regulating tide gates (SRTs), culverts, docks and piers, bridges and causeways). However, primary 
features with multiple paths were only segmented once for the water crossing feature (e.g., railways with 
multiple tracks) to prevent overinflation of water crossing feature lengths and counts. Additionally, 
water crossing features were attributed with their crossing type as “Stream/River,” “Ocean,” or “Not 
visible/unknown,” and by the stream name, if applicable. Lengths of water crossing features were 
summarized by their transecting length as mapped with polylines. Features were then classified by their 
impacts on tidal connectivity, with intact primary features being classified as complete tidal restrictions, 
and water crossings being classified as unrestricted, partial, significant, or complete tidal restrictions 
based on their feature type and status using a rule-based classification (Figure 5 and Table 3).

Tidally influenced extent  
Polygons describing tidally influenced habitat extents were developed using the 2022 draft tidal 
exceedance polygons from Pacific Marine and Estuarine Fish Habitat Partnership (PMEP) and our 
mapped tidal restriction features. To exclude neritic and ocean zones from the PMEP extent, we clipped 
the tidal exceedance polygons to exclude areas that were 1,000-foot seaward of the Beach Strategies 
NGF shoreline. This buffer width was selected to include all potential tidally influenced extents and 
portions of shallow and deeper nearshore tidally influenced habitats that could potentially contain 
manmade tidal restriction features (e.g., docks and piers that extend seaward). We then ran a union 
function to segment the working PMEP wetland extent with the NWIFC Statewide Intertidal Areas 
dataset (NWIFC 2023), to classify intertidal areas into nearshore deep and shallow habitats as described 
in Table 4. The NWIFC dataset allowed us to define seaward boundaries whereas the PMEP provided us

4). The remaining areas were classified generally as the potential tidally influenced extent, which were

Data review and validation  
To facilitate the review, draft tidal restriction polyline and tidally influenced extent polygon data layers 
were uploaded to an ESRI ArcGIS web map hosted by WDFW. Reviewers were provided with a point 
data layer, where they were asked to add comments on restriction and tidally influenced features’ 
existence, type, connectivity impacts, and fish passage. Regional experts from county, state, local, and 
federal agencies were identified using web searches and public communications and outreach, including 
the PSEMP Salmonid Workgroup, and we made direct requests to potential reviewers to review and 
provide feedback on tidal restriction and tidally influenced extent layers. To streamline the review 
process, reviewers were asked to examine features within their area of knowledge as opposed to all 
features within a given area. Unlike the large river delta mapping effort, we did not plan or conduct field 
validation for this phase. Comments received during the regional review were used to update feature 
attributes as appropriate, and the updates and source of information were recorded (see Appendix B: 
Mapping methods and data structure).  
Compiling and sharing final spatial databases 
This project produced the following spatial database products: (1) Original regional datasets compiled 
during data discovery with Unique IDs to link features to final data products; (2) Tidal restriction 
features (polylines) along the Puget Sound nearshore derived from regional data and remote sensing, 
with feature type and connectivity classifications; and (3) Tidally influenced (polygons) extents along 
the nearshore tidal habitats attributed by connectivity ratings from tidal restriction features. These spatial 
databases were published in ESRI ArcGIS Online compatible formats to support data use among a 
diverse audience, as well as a geodatabase platform, which supports point, polyline, and polygon 
features to organize, maintain, store, and develop project data. All spatial database products were 
published in the WA State Plane South NAD 83 HARN (EPSG 2927) coordinate reference system, the 
Washington State standard. Conformance with this standard will ensure consistency across the region 
and provide consistent reporting that can be used to develop and maintain a regional spatial database. 
These spatial databases will be available through WDFW’s ArcOnline mapping application that will be 
available to the public, and links to the data are also provided through PRISM and Salish Sea 
Restoration Platform.

RESULTS AND FINDINGS 
Regional data synthesis 
Thirty-six data portals were identified as potentially containing relevant data, of which 20 were used to 
download vector datasets (Appendix A: Data Discovery Log). Three other data portals were used to 
access reference raster and vector data. A total of 49 spatial datasets containing 267 vector layers were 
identified in data searches for the Large River Deltas and Puget Sound nearshore related to tidally 
influenced nearshore habitat and geomorphology, infrastructure and tidal restrictions, and hydrography 
(Appendix A: Data Discovery Log). Data coverage ranged from locally specific data sets (e.g., 
individual deltas, counties, or diking districts) to those with coverage for the West Coast of the United 
States, and included data sets with features describing tidal restriction, tidally influenced habitat, 
shoreline, floodplain, and channel features.  
Tidal Restrictions 
A total of 3,149 kilometers of tidal restriction features, including 363 kilometers of water crossing 
features, were digitized within the nearshore mapping extent (Table 6). Most of the tidal restriction 
features were composed of armor/fill (32%), roads (50%), docks/piers (10%), or railways (6%) with 4% 
of the total length of mapped features being classified as only water crossing features. Most of the tidal 
restriction features were classified as completely restricting tidal connectivity (86%) with an additional 
10% classified as partial restrictions (Table 6). Approximately 1% of the tidal restriction features were 
classified as either significant restrictions or unrestricted features, while 2% were classified as having 
unknown connectivity (Table 6) Approximately 1% of the mapped features were classified as removed, 
abandoned, or breached, with a large majority (0.4%) classified as not restricting tidal connectivity 
(unrestricted). Some were classified as partially restricting tidal connectivity (0.09%) or significantly 
restricting tidal connectivity (0.18%), and completely restricted (0.27%). However, 0.42% of these 
removed features were classified as having unknown connectivity (Table 6). Approximately 1134 
kilometers of dikes and levees, roads, unmapped roads, and railways were also classified as armored. 
Approximately 90% of the total length of mapped tidal restriction features were included in regional 
data sources, which translated to 316 kilometers of missing features that were identified and manually 
digitized using aerial imagery. Among the feature types, boat ramps, water crossing features associated 
with dikes and levees were the most common features manually digitized (missing from regional data)

Tidally Influenced Extent 
A total of 174,150 hectares of current and potential tidally influenced habitat were mapped and 
classified along the Puget Sound nearshore, not including areas within the previously mapped large river 
deltas (Hall et al. 2021). Nearshore deep and nearshore shallow polygons with partially restricted 
(influenced by docks and piers) and unrestricted ratings accounted for combined total of 161,470 
hectares of the mapped polygon area (93%). Among the remaining 7% of mapped potential tidal extent 
landward of these nearshore shallow and nearshore deep habitats, 10,192 hectares were classified as 
unrestricted tidal habitat, 347 hectares as partially restricted tidal habitat, 211 hectares as significantly 
restricted tidal habitat, 1,438 hectares as completely restricted non-tidal habitat, 284 hectares as 
developed (non-tidal), and 209 hectares with unknown connectivity. This translates to a total of 10,750 
hectares of current tidally influenced habitat extent (unrestricted tidal, partially restricted tidal, and 
significantly restricted tidal), and 1,646 hectares of potential habitat (completely restricted non-tidal and 
unknown tidal connectivity) within the potential tidally influenced habitat extent landward of the 
nearshore deep and shallow habitat polygons.   
Among the Lead Entity areas, we also observed variation in the degree to which potential tidally 
influenced habitat extent was influenced by tidal restriction features (Figure 9). Overall, most of the 
tidally influenced habitat mapped from our tidal restriction features was classified as unrestricted tidally 
influenced habitat among the Lead Entity areas (Figure 9). However, Island, Pierce, and Stillaguamish 
were notable exceptions from the overall regional pattern with greater proportions of mapped habitat 
being classified with some degree of tidal restriction (Figure 9). Island Lead Entity area had the most 
completely restricted tidally influenced extents (993 ha) mapped while Kennedy-Goldsborough had the 
least (0.4 ha). West Sound had the most significantly restricted extent (61 ha) and Duwamish-Green had 
the least significantly restricted extent (0.5 ha). Nooksack had the most partially restricted tidal extent 
(131 ha) and Cedar-Sammamish had the least (0.4 ha). North Olympic Peninsula had the largest 
unrestricted tidal habitat extent (3,482 ha) and the Stillaguamish had the least unrestricted tidal habitat 
extent (2 ha). West Sound also had the most unknown connectivity habitat (61 ha), and Snohomish had 
the least (0.1 ha) classified as having unknown connectivity (Figure 9).

Among the 41 comments regarding tidal restrictions, 22 comments identified features that were missing 
from our database, and this resulted in approximately 0.12 kilometers of tidal restriction features that 
were added based on regional reviewer comments. Fourteen comments confirmed our classifications, 
and therefore no changes were made, and three comments were about feature location being incorrect, 
resulting in those features being manually corrected. Two comments indicated that the feature had been 
updated (either added or removed) since the time of mapping due to restoration. For the feature that was 
added since time of mapping, we manually created a new tidally influenced extent polygon and the 
feature was created and the comment was retained in the review attribute section; previous 
classifications for connectivity and feature type were not filled out. For the feature that was removed 
since time of mapping, the connectivity impact was changed to unrestricted and the water crossing 
feature status was changed to removed. The previous connectivity rating and status were recorded. 
Among the 35 comments regarding tidally influenced extent features, 11 comments indicated that the 
feature was misclassified and these features were updated per reviewer comments. Two comments 
confirmed our classifications of tidally influenced extent, and one comment was regarding a feature that 
was added since time of mapping. For that comment, a new feature was created and the comment was 
retained in the review attribute section. Previous classifications for connectivity and feature type were 
not filled out. In total, 27 tidal restriction polyline features and 14 tidally influenced extent polygon 
features were updated or corrected in response to reviewer comments.  
We also received a number of comments (21 comments) related to our initial attempts at classifying 
tidally influenced extent feature types by geomorphic or functional habitat types (e.g., barrier lagoons, 
closed lagoons, etc.). This revealed some confusion and inconsistency in our initial geomorphic feature 
type classifications. After consultation with our TAG and project partners, we decided to rely on our 
landscape connectivity classifications at this phase of the mapping. However, we did develop a 
preliminary classification system to consider in future application of these data (see Appendix E:

embayment features to support continued development, refinement, and implementation of habitat 
classifications using the spatial data produced by this project.

DISCUSSION 
Our remote sensing-based approach was a reasonably efficient and accurate method for creating an 
updated and consistent regional data layer of tidal restriction features and tidally influenced habitat 
extents for the Puget Sound nearshore. By leveraging existing regional data and synthesizing spatial 
information with aerial imagery and reference data layers, we were able to remove redundant features, 
correct alignment errors, update geometries to reflect current conditions, and map features originally 
missing from regional data. The spatial data produced by this effort in combination with the previously 
mapped large river deltas (ESRP Project #18-2250) represents the most up to date, complete, and 
consistent spatial datasets describing tidal restriction features, the extent of tidally influenced habitat, 
and the degree to which tidal connectivity is influenced by tidal restriction features for the Puget Sound 
region. 
In total, we mapped 3,149 kilometers of tidal restriction features and 174,150 hectares of potential 
tidally influenced habitat using regional data and remote sensing for the Puget Sound nearshore. Most of 
the tidal restriction features mapped in this effort were in existing regional data sets, but approximately 
18% were not previously included in regional data sources and were identified using aerial imagery or 
regional reviewer comments. Interestingly, the percentage of features that were mapped from aerial 
imagery and not in regional datasets for nearshore habitats was lower compared to the previous mapping 
effort for large river deltas (where about 46% of the features were missing from regional data) (Hall et 
al. 2021). While a large proportion of the features were already included in regional data, a significant 
part of our effort was consolidating and aligning features to match the aerial imagery. We manually 
adjusted the alignment for over 92% (2,902 kilometers) of the tidal restriction features in the database, 
which was largely focused on realigning armored banks, roads and unmapped roads, and delineating 
overwater structure features (e.g., docks and piers). This represents a significant improvement in the 
accuracy, completeness, and consistency of regional data describing tidal restriction features.  
Most of the mapped tidal restriction features were classified as complete restrictions for tidal 
connectivity (86%), which was mostly related to the dominance of roads and railways among the 
primary feature types mapped along the nearshore. This was consistent with the findings of the large 
river delta mapping effort (Hall et al. 2021), although in contrast to delta habitats, we found that most 
restrictions ran parallel to the nearshore and PMEP extent and did not intersect or disconnect tidally 
influenced areas, but instead directly truncated the PMEP tidal extent. We initially focused our mapping 
PAGE 39
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  33 
on all features with a 200-ft buffer of the PMEP or within the Beach Strategies NGF polygon, however, 
given the majority of this area was developed, we refined our mapping methods to focus on features 
directly interacting with or abutting the shoreline and PMEP extent, and major permanent infrastructure, 
such as roadways and railways, since these features would be important to consider when planning or 
identifying restoration opportunities. This was consistent with the delta methods, and helped to 
streamline mapping, given the extensive restriction features and development present along the 
nearshore. 
While it is useful to understand the relative lengths of tidal restriction features by their connectivity 
impact ratings, this does not directly translate to the degree to which tidal connectivity is restricted with 
respect to the potential extent of tidally influenced area. To do this, we used our mapped tidal restriction 
features to “carve up” the potential extent of tidal influence from the PMEP extent, and attribute these 
polygons with connectivity ratings from our tidal restriction features. In doing this, we found that most 
of the extent of potential tidal influence along the nearshore was classified as unrestricted tidal habitat 
(80%, excluding nearshore deep and shallow habitat extents). Similar to the tidal restrictions, this also 
contrasts with the findings of our previous mapping efforts in the large river deltas where most of the 
habitat was classified as having some degree of tidal restriction (partial, significant, or completely 
restricted) (Hall et al. 2021). Among the 20% of the mapped tidally influenced habitat landward of the 
nearshore shallow and deep polygon extents, partially restricted tidal habitat accounted for 3%, 
significantly restricted tidal, developed, and unknown connectivity habitat each accounted for 2% of the 
mapped extent, and 11% was classified as completely restricted non-tidal habitat.  
The areas classified as completely restricted non-tidal habitat represent potential tidally influenced 
habitat that could be the focus of restoration or recovery planning. However, it is important to note that 
the PMEP extent used as our basis of mapping represents the modelled maximum extent of tidal 
flooding based on current topography and extreme tidal water levels (PMEP 2018; Brophy et al. 2019). 
Therefore, what we describe as potential tidally influenced habitat extent (completely restricted non-
tidal), does not represent historical extent given that elevation models were derived from modern 
datasets and already integrate some degree of fill and development in the landscape. In addition, we 
noted that there was little difference between what we classified as feature connectivity (based on the 
tidal restriction features that immediately surround a polygon) and landscape connectivity (net tidal 
connectivity to a polygon considering all downstream or seaward features). This contrasts with the large 
PAGE 40
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  34 
river delta habitat mapping effort (Hall et al. 2021), and may be due to the comparatively infrequent 
direct intersection of tidal restriction features and potential tidal influence extent along the nearshore. 
Therefore, we recommend that practitioners primarily use landscape connectivity ratings when 
evaluating the extent of tidally influenced habitats and the degree to which they are impact by tidal 
restrictions given that the landscape connectivity rating accounts for the potential impacts of 
downstream tidal restriction features. However, we included both feature and landscape connectivity 
ratings in the dataset to support other uses where information on the effects of tidal restrictions 
immediately surrounding a polygon are desired.  
Given that the data produced by this effort are intended to support regional salmon recovery planning 
and habitat status and trends monitoring programs (like PSP Vital Sign Indicators and Salmon Habitat 
Indicators program), we also summarized regional patterns among Lead Entity (LE) areas. LE areas are 
similar to Puget Sound sub-basins and counties but are grouped by stocks within a watershed, and are 
managed by a Lead Entity group for salmon recovery plans in Washington state. However, these LE 
areas can also be generally viewed as geographical areas to other agencies and comanagers, and the 
spatial nature of the data facilitates summarizing using any geographical boundaries (e.g., watersheds, 
counties, marine basins, etc.). As expected given regional gradients in land use within the region, we 
observed variation in the lengths of tidal restriction features and degree to which tidally influenced 
extents were impacted by tidal restriction features among Lead Entity areas. This was consistent with the 
previous large river delta mapping effort (Hall et al. 2021) and is reflective of gradients in the degree to 
which land use and development have impacted the landscape at a regional scale. From this effort, we 
note that the Island Lead Entity area had the most completely restricted tidally influenced extents (993 
ha) while Kennedy-Goldsborough had the least (0.4 ha). North Olympic Peninsula had the most 
unrestricted tidal habitat (3,482 ha) and the Stillaguamish had the least unrestricted habitat (2 ha). West 
Sound had the most restrictions mapped (649 kilometers), of which most were complete restrictions 
(550 kilometers). The Stillaguamish Lead Entity area had the least length of tidal restriction features 
mapped among the Lead Entity areas (9.5 kilometers). These comparisons exclude the Nisqually Lead 
Entity area given that nearly the entire area was mapped during the large river delta mapping phase (Hall 
et al. 2021). We provide detailed tabular summaries for each Lead Entity area to support regional 
salmon recovery planning and habitat status and trends monitoring programs in Appendix C: Results. 
Applications and next steps 
PAGE 41
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  35 
In combination with our previous mapping efforts for Puget Sound’s large river deltas (Hall et al. 2021), 
completion of this phase of mapping has created the most complete, consistent, and up to date spatial 
database of tidal restriction features and tidally influenced habitat for the Puget Sound region. These 
datasets directly addressed a significant regional data gap by providing consistent regional scale data 
that can be used to inform Puget Sound salmon recovery planning and evaluation in several key ways 
that include: (1) habitat status and trends monitoring and evaluation, (2) refining and reporting on 
recovery targets and progress towards recovery, and (3) identification and prioritization of restoration 
opportunities. 
Habitat status and trends monitoring and evaluation: The extent of tidally connected habitats has been 
identified as a limiting factor for Puget Sound Chinook populations, and regional recovery strategies 
have identified restoration of tidal connectivity as a priority for the recovery of Chinook in the Puget 
Sound region (Beamer et al., 2005; PSP 2016; Beechie et al. 2017). Tracking the status and trends of 
tidally influenced and estuarine habitat extent is an important component of recovery planning and 
evaluation (Beechie et al. 2017). Regionally consistent spatial datasets that can be regularly updated are 
key to habitat status and trends monitoring and evaluation, and our spatial database framework provides 
the first step towards this. With completion of mapping for nearshore habitats and previously completed 
large river delta habitats, our spatial database provides consistent spatial data that can be used to 
describe the extent and composition of tidal restriction features and the extent of tidally influenced 
habitats and the degree to which tidal connectivity is impaired at a regional scale circa 2017-2022. The 
large river delta datasets have already been used to update reporting on habitat status and trends 
indicators for important regional recovery planning and evaluation programs including: Puget Sound 
Partnership (PSP) Vital Sign (VS) and Salmon Habitat Indicators (SHI) (Ramirez, 2019a, b; PSP et al. 
2022), National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s (NOAA) Salmon Habitat Status and Trends 
Monitoring Program (SHSTMP) (SHSTMP 2018), and Puget Sound Ecosystem Monitoring Program 
(PSEMP) Spatial Data Work Group (SDWG) (PSEMP 2022), and we anticipate that these new data that 
complete data coverage at the Puget Sound scale will be used in the same way.  
With the completion of this nearshore mapping effort, spatial data are now available to support habitat 
status and trends monitoring and reporting for nearshore habitats that can provide important non-natal 
rearing and refuge habitats for juvenile Chinook Salmon (e.g., pocket estuaries, lagoons, embayments, 
and other tidally influenced wetland and channel habitats) (Beamer et al., 2005). In fact, the PSP Vital 
PAGE 42
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  36 
Sign and Salmon Habitat Indicators program has been working towards refining and updating habitat 
status and trends indicators and reporting for these habitats (area and count of accessible pocket estuary 
and embayments habitat). The data produced from this mapping effort will directly inform the 
refinement of those indicators in coordination with another ongoing Estuary and Salmon Restoration 
Program Learning Project (Embayment Strategies for Restoration and Protection PRISM #22-1767). 
Although we initially attempted to classify polygons into functional or geomorphic habitat types, more 
work is needed to develop a regionally consistent habitat classification system that is useful for recovery 
planning and evaluation. To help with this, we have developed a preliminary habitat classification tree 
(see Appendix E: Proposed Decision Tree for habitats) to support development of a regionally consistent 
classification of the tidally influenced polygons mapped by this project to support habitat status and 
trends monitoring and reporting for nearshore habitats important to salmon recovery.  
The ability to update the spatial database and track changes over time are also key to habitat status and 
trends monitoring and reporting. The spatial database we developed includes fields to track changes in 
both tidal restriction and tidally influenced extent features. For example, status and modification fields 
allow the database to be updated when features are removed, breached, or modified through restoration 
actions or natural change while retaining information on the previous feature type and geometry of the 
features within the database (see Appendix B: Mapping methods and data structure). These fields can 
also be used to update and track changes to the data as additional regional reviews or comments are 
received, which we recommend continued regional review given that there are many features still 
flagged as unknown or with low certainty. In both cases, guidelines will be developed in coordination 
with the Puget Sound Partnership to maintain and update the database to support use of these datasets 
for regional habitat status and trends monitoring and reporting. However, we do note that landscape 
connectivity ratings have been manually attributed and are not dynamically enforced with networking at 
this time, and therefore updates to connectivity ratings will need to be manually updated (for both the 
feature directly changed and any associated features landward of that feature) as the database is 
maintained in the future.   
Refining and reporting on recovery targets and progress towards recovery: As noted above, these 
datasets provide regionally consistent spatial data needed to report on indicators of habitat status and 
trends. By integrating tidal connectivity ratings into our dataset, these data allow for habitat to be 
quantified with respect to varying degrees of tidal connectivity. We have already seen updates to the 
PAGE 43
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  37 
PSP VS and SHI reporting protocols to include reporting by tidal connectivity rating rather than just 
accessible area based on our previous efforts in the large river deltas (Ramirez, 2019a, b; PSP et al. 
2022), and this provides more information to inform recovery targets and evaluation of progress towards 
recovery. We envision the same approach being adopted for developing, refining, and reporting on the 
PSP’s VS and SHI nearshore embayment and habitat indicators (e.g., area and count of accessible 
pocket estuary and embayments habitat). Similar to our recommendations for the large river deltas, we 
also recommend that recovery targets for nearshore embayment and pocket estuary habitats be 
developed at watershed or Lead Entity area scales. This allows recovery planning to account for relative 
degree to which embayment or pocket estuary habitats are currently available, the degree to which those 
are impaired with respect to tidal connectivity, and potential tidally influenced habitat – which varies 
among the Lead Entity areas due to natural variations in geomorphology as well as land use history.      
Identification and prioritization of restoration opportunities: The spatial nature of our database allows 
practitioners and stakeholders to query tidal restriction polyline or tidally influenced extent features by 
feature types, connectivity ratings, shoreform, or geographic area (e.g., Lead Entity area). Furthermore, 
polygon features that describe the extent of tidally influenced habitats or potential extent are also linked 
to the associated tidal restriction features that surround the polygon feature. This allows the user to 
identify a potential restoration site (e.g., culvert removal replaced with a bridge or removing armored 
features, etc.) and the features that impact tidal connectivity to specific polygons to inform restoration 
planning. To demonstrate the application of the spatial database for the identification and prioritization 
of restoration opportunities, consider a hypothetical example of a practitioner that seeks to identify and 
prioritize opportunities to increase the amount of tidally influenced habitat by restoring tidal 
connectivity to areas that are currently completely disconnected. In this case, the practitioner can use the 
spatial database to select geographic areas of interest (if desired), and then query the polygon dataset to 
select features that are classified as Completely Restricted Non-Tidal habitat. These features represent 
areas that are within an appropriate elevation to be tidally influenced but are currently completely 
disconnected from tidal flooding due to tidal restriction features or natural topography. The selected 
features can then be filtered or prioritized using information within the database (e.g., shoreform or 
surrounding tidal restriction feature types) or by joining with other datasets (e.g., parcel data to provide 
ownership information, accessibility via roads or other transportation infrastructure). For example, the 
practitioner may want to filter or prioritize the selected polygons to those that are within publicly held 
lands, with the assumption that those sites would be easier to restore. Additionally, the practitioner may 
PAGE 44
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  38 
want to filter or prioritize by the types of tidal restriction features that are associated with the polygons. 
For example, addressing tidal connectivity at already existing water crossing features that completely 
restrict tidal connectivity (e.g., traditional flap gate or floodgates) through restoration or enhancement 
actions may be easier or lower cost than sites that would require extensive excavation or channel 
construction to restore or enhance tidal connectivity to a site. In this way, the practitioner can use the 
spatial data to identify and prioritize restoration opportunities to address specific objectives (in this case, 
increasing the amount of tidally connected habitat). Another example could be using the spatial data to 
summarize by drift cell types to support the identification and prioritization of restoration opportunities 
from a drift cell perspective (e.g., understanding possible co-benefits of restoring both tidal connectivity 
and sediment supply or evaluating opportunities for restoration of tidal restrictions at the drift cell 
scale). To support the identification and prioritization of restoration planning, we envision working with 
ESRP and PSP to develop widgets and tools for the online map hosted by WDFW.  
Opportunities for updating related organizational data to align recovery efforts: In addition, our 
spatial data provides information on water crossing features that can directly inform updating regional 
databases such as the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife (WDFW) Fish Passage and 
Diversion Screening Inventory (FPDSI) database (WDFW 2020). The WDFW FPDSI represents a 
significant regional resource for identifying and funding restoration opportunities to improve fish 
passage and access to habitats. Inventories and evaluations of fish passage at WCFs in tidally influenced 
habitats has been a critical data gap in the FPDSI. Our database identifies numerous WCF in tidally 
influenced areas previously not included in regional data, including the FPDSI database, which can be 
used to significantly improve the completeness of the regional inventory. Therefore, these data can be 
used to inform future assessments to evaluate and restore fish passage issues associated with water 
crossing features in tidally influenced habitats by providing an updated spatial inventory of potential 
features to evaluate (WDFW 2019a, b).

APPENDIX B: MAPPING METHODS AND DATA 
STRUCTURE 
Tidal restriction regional data processing and mapping 
The processing software QGIS (QGIS 2024) was used to visualize and process all datasets. An original 
copy of all datasets was retained and a working version of each of the datasets was projected to the 
project coordinate reference system (CRS), WA State Plane South NAD 83 HARN (EPSG 2927) and 
saved as a geopackage (a QGIS layer package that allows for storage of large datasets of multiple 
geometries and data types). To maximize storage and usability, the projected datasets were clipped to a 
656 ft (200 m) buffer of the shoreline study area and the fix geometry tool was used to address potential 
errors generated from the projecting and clipping process.  
Many of the original data sources contain detailed information that may be useful for some applications 
but weren’t included in the streamlined database structure. Therefore, we performed the same process 
used in the mapping of large river delta tidal restrictions and delta tidal wetlands and created a Unique 
ID for all features contained in the original data sources by combining the original dataset name and row 
number. This allows the use of the Unique ID to link the features to their original data source by using a 
join operation in GIS (Table B.10).  
All projected, clipped, and Unique ID attributed datasets were then loaded into a QGIS project with 
aerial imagery and other reference data sources for use in mapping tidal restriction and polygon features. 
The regional data review and remote sensing protocol included several steps that are described in the 
following sections:  
(1) synthesis of redundant features and correcting spatial alignment,  
(2) updating removed or modified features,  
(3) digitizing missing features,  
(4) classification of feature types and certainty, and  
(5) classification of feature tidal connectivity and certainty.  
(1) Synthesis of redundant features 
Regional data varied in their geometry (lines versus points) used to depict tidal restrictions (e.g., 
culverts, levees, bridges) as well as their spatial resolution and accuracy. In addition, many features were 
PAGE 57
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  51 
captured or described by multiple data layers that had differing alignments within small spatial scales, 
creating problems for regional scale analysis and selection of appropriate data layers. Such variations in 
regional data are shown in Figure B.10 to illustrate our remote sensing approach to synthesizing and 
updating regional data layers into a consistent spatial database. We reviewed redundancies (i.e., the 
same physical feature included in multiple datasets) and gave precedence to; (1) local datasets over 
regional data (e.g., county vs state datasets) and (2) datasets with higher spatial accuracy relative to 
aerial imagery. All qualifying features from the regional data were copied into the CFS tidal restriction 
data geopackage layer, and their source was retained in the feature Unique ID. 
(2) Digitizing missing features 
Visible features not included in the regional datasets were digitized at a scale of 1:1,000 and the source 
was recorded as aerial imagery to indicate the feature was digitized from remote sensing. Placement of 
features was based on aerial imagery, but supplemental data (e.g., USGS 3DEP hill-shaded DEM, 
PMEP tidal exceedance polygons, LiDAR, or oblique shoreline imagery) were used where available to 
inform placement where features were obscured by cover or shadows or couldn’t otherwise be seen in 
the aerial imagery. Features were also given a certainty rating for presence; features not clearly visible in 
the imagery were flagged as low certainty and for further evaluation in the field or in regional review 
(Table B.10). 
(3) Correcting alignments and updating features 
We modified regional data features as needed to align them to the feature center point as visible in the 
aerial imagery. All manual feature corrections were performed at a scale of 1:1,000, and corrections 
were recorded for each feature to indicate the type of correction. Figure B.10 shows an example of the 
synthesized and corrected regional data layer outputs from the compiled regional data. Features were 
also attributed with status as “Present,” “Breached,” or “Removed,” if indicated in the regional data, or 
if observed in the aerial imagery (Table B.9). If a feature was mapped as present in the regional data, but 
was observed to have been removed, breached, or modified in the aerial imagery, the feature alignment 
was maintained and the previous feature type and connectivity was noted, but the feature type, 
connectivity, and status were updated to reflect the imagery. Restoration efforts were incorporated into 
the tidal restrictions database when documented in the regional data or visible in the aerial imagery. 
(4) Classifying feature types and certainty 
PAGE 58
 Final Report 
  Cramer Fish Sciences  52 
Features were classified using a two-tiered nested structure that included attributing a primary and water 
crossing feature type (where applicable), which are defined and described in detail in Table 2. Feature 
types were identified using regional data or aerial imagery. The first tier described the primary feature 
type and included roads and railways, dikes and levees, armored or filled banks, boat ramps, and 
footpaths if applicable.  
The second tier described the water crossing structure type, if applicable. Water crossings included 
bridges, causeways, piers, and docks, floodgates and pumpstations, self-regulating tide gates, culverts, 
pipes, open crossings (e.g., breaches or removed features), unknown, or NULL if no water crossing 
feature was associated with a primary feature (Table 2). Water crossings not mapped in the regional data 
were identified in the aerial imagery by the evidence of flow or tidal connectivity under a solid feature. 
If the water crossing was not part of a primary feature (e.g., agricultural bridges and culverts without a 
graded road), then the primary feature was classified as NULL. If a water crossing existed as part of a 
primary feature, the primary feature line was segmented on either side of the crossing (using the mapped 
regional data location and aerial imagery as reference), and the segment was classified by both the 
primary feature (e.g., roads, railways, dikes, levees, armored banks) and the water crossing feature type 
(e.g., floodgates, self-regulating tide gates (SRTs), culverts, bridges and causeways). Water crossing 
features that were included in the WDFW FPDSI database (WDFW 2020) were also attributed with fish 
passage status information and the unique site ID (reflective of information available on Date). All tidal 
restriction features were also classified as high or low certainty for the feature type classification, based 
on if the feature type was listed in the regional data or clearly visible in the imagery (high) or if the 
feature type was determined from the imagery (low). Additionally, many armored banks were mapped in 
the regional data as adjacent to roads and railways running along the shoreline, however these are 
essentially a single feature, the road or railway with armor to protect it (Figure B.10). To account for 
these features, we added an additional attribute of “Armored,” so features adjacent to the shoreline could 
be mapped as a single feature of their primary type (for example, railway) with armoring, as opposed to 
two separate features (one line for the railway, one line for the armor). 
We also documented overall location data for restriction features by adding attributes for the Chinook 
Recovery Watershed, the study extent strata the feature is located in (e.g., seaward of the Beach 
Strategies NGF, within the Beach Strategies NGF 0-200 ft polygon, within the PMEP extent or mapped 
tidal wetland extent, and/or within the 200 ft buffer), and the Beach Strategies NGF shoreform (CGS

2017). This will allow compatibility and comparison with other nearshore programs that utilize the 
Beach Strategies data.  
(5) Classification of feature tidal connectivity and certainty 
Tidal connectivity was classified based on a combination of feature type information, interpretation of 
aerial imagery, and supplemental data sources. Given that aerial imagery interpretation limited our 
ability to classify tidal connectivity, we used general categorical classifications of tidal connectivity 
impacts based on the feature type. These classifications were based on the approach used in the lower 
Columbia River estuary for mapping and classifying tidal habitats based on tidal restriction features and 
function (LCEP 2009) and are consistent with those used in the large river delta mapping effort (Hall et 
al. 2021). These ratings included unrestricted, partially restricted, significantly restricted, completely 
restricted, or unknown restriction to tidal connectivity based on their feature type and status (see Table 3 
for detailed definitions and descriptions). Certainty ratings for tidal connectivity classifications were 
also assigned and were based on feature type classifications and certainties, aerial imagery, and 
supplemental data evidence. 
(6) Updating features from regional reviewers’ comments 
During dataset review, regional reviewers provided comments on features where they had additional 
knowledge, such as restoration activities, removals, or natural events that could impact a feature’s status, 
type, or connectivity. After the review, comments were categorized into verification categories for both 
tidal restrictions and tidally influenced extents datasets to track changes: 
• feature misclassified in database,  
• unknown feature identified,  
• feature missing from database,  
• feature does not exist,  
• feature has been added (since time of mapping),  
• feature has been updated (from restoration or development), 
• feature removed (from restoration or development),  
• feature classification verified,  
• feature extent or location incorrect, and  
• other.

  Cramer Fish Sciences  54 
Features classified as misclassified in the database were updated per reviewer comments, with 
previous status, type, or connectivity recorded. Features marked as missing from the database or 
added (since time of mapping) were newly created, with reviewer comments retained and a note about 
the edits. For classification verification, no changes were made, but comments were retained with an 
edit note. Features categorized as updated (from restoration or development) or removed (from 
restoration or development) were revised accordingly, with previous status and tidal connectivity 
attributes recorded. Features categorized as feature extent or location incorrect were manually 
adjusted, with reviewer comments retained and an edit note added. Comments regarding tidally 
influenced habitat type were categorized as other, with a note indicating these updates will occur in a 
later phase. Features where reviewers confirmed a feature did not exist were categorized as feature 
does not exist and removed from the dataset to avoid confusion with features removed due to restoration 
or development. For unknown feature identified, meaning the feature was not captured through aerial 
imagery or regional data, it was added to the dataset and attributed based on the reviewer’s comment.

Delineating potential tidally influenced extent 
The PMEP extent represents the maximum potential extent of tidally influenced areas based on an 
elevation-based estuary extent model (EBEEM) that combined lidar digital elevation models (DEMs) 
and water level models, but it does not account for tidal connectivity (Brophy et al. 2019). For example, 
areas surrounded by a complete tidal restriction feature like a dike or levee that have elevation lower 
than predicted maximum tidal elevations would be delineated in the PMEP tidal exceedance polygons. 
However, the extents of the PMEP polygons are based on modern digital elevation models which 
exclude areas that have been filled and developed and do not represent the true historical tidal wetland 
extent. 
The tidal restriction feature (primary and water crossing features) network were buffered using a fixed 
buffer width of 10 feet. The buffer was intended to represent the footprint of tidal restriction features in 
lieu of digitizing the entire footprint from aerial imagery, which would also require elevation datasets to 
accurately delineate. We used the difference tool to remove the buffered tidal restriction network from 
the potential tidal extent and segment the resulting tidally influenced polygons. We then used the tidal 
restriction connectivity ratings to attribute connectivity to the resulting tidally influenced extent polygon 
network with feature and landscape tidal connectivity classifications. Tidally influenced extents were 
also segmented and delineated by landscape connectivity.  
Tidally influenced areas were given certainty ratings for connectivity. If the tidal restrictions that 
influenced connectivity to a tidally influenced area had low certainty ratings for connectivity, tidally 
influenced connectivity ratings were also low certainty. All tidally influenced extents delineated by 
PMEP tidal exceedance extent were given high certainty ratings, except where the extent was manually 
corrected using remote sensing or aerial imagery.  
Tidal restriction and tidally influenced area features were assigned unique identifiers and were spatially 
joined to create a crosswalk table relating polygon features to the tidal restrictions that surround them. 
This allows tidally influenced areas to be evaluated by restrictions that surround them, as well as 
allowing tidal restrictions to be evaluated based on their associated tidally influenced areas. This also 
allows for the length of restrictions to be totaled by their connectivity impacts for each individual 
polygon. Methods for generating this crosswalk are available in Appendix D: Digitizing Methods.

APPENDIX D: DIGITIZING METHODS 
(1) General Tidal Restriction Mapping: 
a. Objective. To map tidal restrictions for all of Puget Sound using existing regional data 
and aerial imagery, eliminating duplicate features and correcting misalignments in the 
regional data.  
b. Input layers. Current aerial imagery (e.g., Google Earth, Hexagon Imagery Program 0.3 
m 4-band aerial imagery 2017) and oblique shoreline imagery (DOE 2016) as needed for 
remote sensing and interpretation; elevation data (e.g., LiDAR DEMs or PMEP Tidal 
Exceedance layers) as available or needed to map boundaries of tidal influence; existing 
regional habitat, infrastructure, and hydrology layers. Input layers are required to be in 
Washington State Plane South NAD 83 HARN (EPSG 2927) coordinate system. 
c. Data Inventory. A single vector layer will contain the mapped features for tidal 
restrictions. Input layers are required to be in Washington State Plane South NAD 83 
HARN (EPSG 2927) coordinate system. The data entry form is shown in Table B.10. 
d. Adding polygon features. Polygon features will be copied from existing regional data 
sets and “heads- up digitized” on screen as lines using the mouse to manually align 
features with the aerial imagery or snap the features to other regional data layers, or both. 
All lines should be mapped at a maximum of 1:1,000 scale based on available data sets.

APPENDIX E: PROPOSED DECISION TREE FOR HABITATS 
As mentioned above in the executive summary, we initially attempted to classify potential tidally influenced embayment and lagoonal habitat 
types into functional or geomorphic classifications (e.g., barrier lagoons, closed lagoons, etc.), but it was determined (through regional 
review) that additional coordination is needed to develop a consistent classification schema. Therefore, we propose this draft decision tree and 
plan to work with regional reviewers, technical advisory group, and project partners to establish a consistent classification for these habitat 
types.
