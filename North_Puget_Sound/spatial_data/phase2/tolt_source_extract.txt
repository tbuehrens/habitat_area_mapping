Beyond water supply and hydropower, the SFT River provides important habitat to many fish and 
wildlife. There are five native anadromous species of salmon in the Lower SFT River, including summer 
and winter steelhead. The salmon species, with the exception of summer steelhead, inhabit the first 2.5 
miles of the Lower SFT River to spawn and rear. The summer steelhead, one of only a handful of 
populations identified in Puget Sound, are able to navigate the Lower SFT River’s steep mile-long canyon 
reach, made up of cascades and chutes, to access an additional four miles of river between the top of 
the canyon reach and the SFT Falls, a double waterfall with a combined height of over 70 feet. The SFT 
Dam is located approximately 0.5 miles upstream of the SFT Falls.  
From the SFT Reservoir, water is released from a service conduit and/or spillway immediately below the 
SFT Dam back into the Lower SFT River and is also routed through a Penstock (a pipe that conveys water 
to a turbine) approximately 5 miles to the Powerhouse. Water is directed from the Powerhouse and 
then discharged into a Regulating Basin before being conveyed to the Tolt Water Treatment Facility for 
water supply purposes. Occasionally to support dam safety and/or flood management objectives (less 
than 5% of the year), water is also discharged from the Powerhouse directly into the Lower SFT River, 
along with discharge to the Regulating Basin.  
Power from the Project is transmitted via an approximately 9-mile-long City Light Electric Transmission 
Line to the Stillwater Substation in a shared utility corridor with SPU’s Water Transmission Line. 
The City owns the majority of land necessary for the operation and maintenance of the Project (i.e., 
FERC Project Boundary) except for small sections of the Tolt Access Road to the Regulating Basin for 
which it has a right-of way (ROW). There are no federal lands within the FERC Project Boundary.  
Regarding lands in the Project Vicinity that are outside the FERC Project Boundary, two-thirds of the 
watershed above the SFT Dam, referred to as the Upper SFT Watershed, is forested land that is owned 
and managed by the City; the remaining one-third of the Upper SFT Watershed is owned and managed 
by the U.S. Forest Service (USFS). Notably, the City-owned lands are closed to public access to protect 
the quality of the public water supply per City Code 21.12.050. Most of the land surrounding the Lower 
SFT River, downstream of the SFT Dam, is privately owned and managed as a working forest.  
The Current FERC License 
The current license for the Project was issued by FERC in March 1984. The Tulalip Tribes and federal and 
state resource agencies filed motions to intervene in the relicensing proceeding, focusing primarily on 
the need for adequate long-term provisions for managing instream flows, habitat restoration, and 
monitoring salmon redds and water temperature. In July 1984, FERC issued a stay of the license pending 
further environmental review. City Light, on behalf of the City, filed a Settlement Agreement with FERC 
in 1988 between the City, the Tulalip Tribes, and federal and state resource agencies. The Settlement 
Agreement included fish and wildlife protection, mitigation, and enhancement (PME) measures and 
post-licensing studies proposed to be incorporated into the license. The Settlement Agreement was 
approved by FERC, its terms and provisions were incorporated into the license, and the stay was lifted in 
1989.

4.1.5 Climate 
This section describes regional climate trends within the Snohomish Watershed. Where data exist, 
descriptions will focus preferentially on the SFT Watershed. Impacts of climate and climate change on 
resource areas will be discussed in relevant subsections.  
4.1.5.1 Current Climate 
The SFT Watershed has a temperate marine climate with warm, dry summers and cool, mild, wet 
winters. Temperatures in the SFT Watershed typically dip below freezing during winter months and 
reach about 25°C (77°F) in the summer months (USGS Gage 12147900, WY 1991 – WY 2023). In the 
Snohomish Watershed, most of the annual precipitation is in the form of winter snow in the humid 
mountainous regions (approximately Elevation 5000 to 7500 feet) and in the form of mixed snow and 
rain in the lower elevations (Elevation 5000 feet to sea level). In the Cascade Range, annual precipitation 
ranges from about 150 to 200 inches (SPU 2011). Average annual precipitation in the SFT Watershed is 
approximately 105 inches (NCEI Gage USC00458508, WY 1996 – WY 2023).  
The largest pattern of natural variability within the global climate system is the El Niño–Southern 
Oscillation (ENSO). There are two phases to this oscillation, denoted as the El Niño phase and the La 
Niña phase. The El Niño phase of the oscillation is characterized by warmer-than-normal temperatures 
in the eastern equatorial Pacific that results in a greater chance of warmer and drier Pacific Northwest 
weather conditions during the winter and spring (Mote et al. 2013). The La Niña phase of the oscillation 
is characterized by cooler-than-normal temperatures in the eastern equatorial Pacific that results in a 
higher chance of a cooler and wetter Pacific Northwest during the winter and spring (Mote et al. 2013). 
ENSO events occur with a frequency of approximately every 2 to 8 years (Brunn et al. 2017). 
“Atmospheric River” is the name given to a specific type of meteorological precipitation event 
characterized by oceanic atmospheric pressure systems that generate narrow, elongated swaths of 
warm, moist air originating in tropical latitudes that carry large amounts of water vapor to mid-latitudes 
of the eastern Pacific Ocean. Atmospheric rivers provide a substantial portion of the western North 
American precipitation, including the Pacific Northwest and SFT Watershed. They can also cause major 
flooding events either directly or because of rain-on-snow events (Dettinger 2011). The West Coast 
experiences on average 24 atmospheric rivers a year, mostly in the late fall and winter (Yang and Hyemi 
2019).  
Puget Sound Convergence Zone (PSCZ) events can also locally affect the Snohomish Watershed, and 
occasionally the SFT Watershed. A PSCZ event occurs when strong westerly winds split around the 
Olympic Mountains and reconverge over Puget Sound, causing narrow bands of precipitation (NOAA 
n.d.). These events typically occur dozens of times per year, and usually in the late spring (Chien and 
Mass, 1997).

4.3.6 Known or Potential Effects 
This section describes the effects on water resources of continued operation of the Project under a new 
license, which include direct, indirect, and cumulative effects as defined by the CEQ regulations at 40 
CFR § 1508.1. Direct and indirect effects are described below. Due to the interconnected nature of the 
hydroelectric and water supply projects, the independent effects of each are difficult to distinguish. 
Therefore, effects of the Tolt Water Supply Project that cannot be easily distinguished from the 
hydroelectric Project are conservatively included as direct and indirect effects. All other additional 
actions and/or events that may incrementally contribute to effects on water resources are described 
under Cumulative Effects, including any effects of the Tolt Water Supply Project that can be 
distinguished from the hydroelectric Project. While cumulative effects under NEPA are Project-related 
effects when added to other past, present, and reasonably foreseeable actions or events, they are listed 
separately from direct and indirect effects in this PAD to distinguish them from such effects. 
4.3.6.1 Direct and Indirect Effects 
Known or potential direct or indirect effects on water resources that may result from continued 
operation of the Project include the following: 
• Instream flows: Flows in the Lower SFT River below the SFT Dam were first modified by the 
construction of the Tolt Water Supply Project in a manner consistent with water storage 
projects and continues to be modified after the addition of the hydroelectric Project. 
Specifically, flow duration curves (Section 4.3.5.3) show that the Project creates more consistent 
flows below the SFT Dam, with reduced magnitude and frequency of high flow events and 
increased base flow in summer. The reduced variability of high flows are dampened by the 
inflow of Lynch Creek to the Lower SFT River and the operation of the RRV, and further 
downstream, they are difficult to observe in the Mainstem Tolt River due to the much larger and 
unregulated flow of the NFT River. 
• Water temperature in the Lower SFT River: During the original licensing, FERC concluded that 
the thermal regime of the Lower SFT River would not be significantly altered by the Project, 
provided that the City properly blends the temperature through water released by the multiple 
level Intake Tower at the SFT Dam.  
• Water temperature in the SFT Reservoir: The temperature of the SFT Reservoir is typical of low 
-elevation, mid-latitude reservoirs. Available data need to be analyzed more thoroughly to 
understand the potential impact of Project operations on temperature in the SFT Reservoir. 
• Turbidity: Under low reservoir elevations, elevated turbidities can be observed in the Lower SFT 
River. The extent or mechanisms for which Project operations affect turbidity are not clear.

4.4.3.1 SFT River Process Domains and Geomorphic Reaches 
Process domains along the SFT River are defined primarily by the boundaries of the different geological 
provinces through which the river flows and the extent of glaciation. The Upper SFT River conveys 
sediment from much of the Upper SFT Watershed into the SFT Reservoir. It can be divided into three 
principal process domains. 
• The headwaters flow out of Crater Lake, a glacial cirque61F61 carved into the north valley wall, 
and are joined by steep alpine tributaries.  
• Once the Upper SFT River reaches the main axis of the glacial valley at RM 16, the river flows 
through a narrow (150-foot to 250-foot wide) inset valley incised 20 to 50 feet into the 
surrounding glacial till plain.  
• From about the confluence with Phelps Creek to the river’s delta into the reservoir, Tolt River 
alluvium fills the whole 1,300-foot valley bottom width. RM alluvial segments of the Upper SFT 
River generally decline in slope from about 0.1 in the upstream portion of the glacial valley (RM 
16) to a minimum of about 0.01 at the delta apex (RM 13).  
The SFT Reservoir fills about the same areas as had been occupied by the ice-marginal lake in the valley 
during the Fraser glaciation (see Section 4.2.5.2). Tributaries to the SFT Reservoir are typically steep with 
small watershed areas, as they flow down glacially-scoured slopes or from small cirque valleys (Section 
4.2.5). Tributary bed material is typically cobble to boulder with some areas of bedrock exposure. 
Typically only the relatively low gradient downstream-most segments of tributaries to the Upper SFT 
River and Reservoir meet the physical criteria for fish habitat (described in detail in Section 4.5.4). 
Below the SFT Dam, the Lower SFT River flows through a moderately to highly confined gorge incised 
into glacial sediment and bedrock and is subject to landslides from the valley walls. The few tributaries 
to the Lower SFT River typically have small drainage areas, as the surrounding glacial plateau is not 
deeply dissected (Sections 4.2.5 and 4.4.7). Valley confinement and gradient control differences 
between transport reaches and response reaches. Relatively steep and confined transport reaches 
support very little storage of bed material sediment, while relatively less confined and gentle response 
reaches support some storage of gravel and cobble-sized material and lateral geomorphic activity. The 
transport reaches are often controlled by bedrock, including the 73-foot SFT Falls at RM 7.8. Bedrock 
segments control the base level of upstream channel segments that are incised into and variably 
confined by surrounding glacial sediment. Response reaches occupy broader valleys and include some 
areas where a floodplain has developed.

4.4.6 Channel Migration 
No detailed historical channel migration or channel migration zone delineation studies have been 
completed along either the Upper or Lower SFT River. Channel migration along the Lower SFT River is 
limited by extensive confinement by glacial and bedrock valley walls, the presence of coarse boulder and 
block lag deposits, and vegetation reinforcing channel banks (Earth Systems and Parametrix 2003), even 
in areas with a broader valley bottom where some features indicating channel past channel migration 
have been observed (Earth Systems and Parametrix 2003). Earth Systems and Parametrix (2003) 
identified moderate or active bank instability along about 4% of the inventoried portion of the Lower 
SFT River banklines (the inventory excluded areas above SFT Falls and in the canyon reach). Reaches 
with observed active bank erosion were lower-gradient alluvial reaches, including SF 7 (7.2% of the 
reach bank length) and SF 6 (4.2%), SF 2 (5.5%), and SF 1 (6.3%), and areas still recovering from mass 
wasting disturbance including SF 10 (6.5%) and SF 9 (4%). Bank erosion responses to the 2009 and 2020 
floods have not been quantitatively evaluated. 
Channel migration zone mapping based on detailed evaluation of historical channel migration rates and 
patterns has been completed for the lower Mainstem Tolt River. Initial mapping was completed by 
Shannon & Wilson Inc. (King County 1991) and subsequently updated by Butler (2017). Butler found that 
average annual channel migration rates vary from 2 to 11 ft/yr. Higher channel migration rates are 
observed in Reaches MS2 and MS3, which have typical erosion rates of 4 to 9 ft/yr, and the lowest rates 
are observed in Reach MS1, MS4, and MS5, which are relatively confined and have erosion rates on the 
order of 1 to 3 ft/ yr (Butler 2017). The mapped channel migration zone covers an approximately 500-
foot-wide swath around the river channel in Reach MS5, nearly spans the valley in Reaches MS2 to MS4 
due to potential avulsion paths along floodplain channels, and covers a nearly 2,000-foot-wide swath 
across the alluvial fan reach (Reach 1).

4.4.8.1 Direct and Indirect Effects 
Known or potential direct or indirect effects on fluvial geomorphology that may result from continued 
operation of the Project include the following: 
• Sediment supply: The SFT Dam and ongoing Project operations have cut off coarse sediment 
supply from the SFT Watershed upstream of the SFT Dam since 1963 (Butler 2017). Coarse 
sediment is needed to maintain spawning habitat and the channel profile in alluvial reaches 
(Section 4.5.2.7). As documented in Section 4.4.4.2, studies were completed under the 
Settlement Agreement to evaluate this effect. Those studies found that there appears to be 
adequate coarse sediment supply from local recruitment downstream of the SFT Dam to sustain 
spawning patches and bars.  
• Channel forming and maintenance flows: Project operations have reduced the magnitude and 
duration of large flows in the Lower SFT River compared to a natural hydrograph (Section 
4.3.5.3). This has likely reduced channel width, reduced bank erosion providing coarse sediment 
and LWD to the channel, and reduced transport capacity for sediment and LWD. This regulation 
of flood flows also reduces the frequency of flows capable of scouring redds (Section 4.4.4.2).  
• Wood supply: The SFT Dam and ongoing Project operations have cut off wood supply from the 
Upper SFT Watershed upstream of the SFT Dam since 1963 (Butler 2017). Limited management 
of wood in the SFT Reservoir (Section 4.4.5.1) suggests this is a relatively small effect. Small 
wood contributes to log jams by racking on existing LWD pieces and log jams, enlarging them 
and increasing their hydraulic roughness (Abbe and Montgomery 2003, Manners et al. 2007).  
These known or potential effects diminish with distance downstream of the SFT Dam and increasing 
additional contributing area supplying these materials. Dramatic reductions in the magnitude of each 
effect occur at the key confluences identified in Table 4-1.

Lower SFT River 
Habitat Overview 
The Lower SFT River provides 7.8 miles of aquatic habitat between its downstream confluence with the 
Mainstem Tolt River and the SFT Falls at RM 7.8 (over 0.5 mile below the SFT Dam) which represents the 
extent of anadromy and is commonly understood by Tribes, WDFW, and other agencies to be a natural 
upstream passage barrier to all fish species (Figure 4-85 and Figure 4-86). This is noted in a 1990 report 
by WDFW which states that the SFT Falls are “well-known” and “constitute a complete block” (Pfeifer 
1990). The SFT Falls are also noted by Tribal oral tradition as preventing migration of fish. In addition, 
the Tulalip Tribes have stated in their Chinook Salmon spawning survey reports that “anadromous 
salmon utilization can occur up to river mile 7.8, where a natural barrier (waterfall) prevents any further 
access.” 
The portion of the Lower SFT that is accessible to upstream fish migrants has distinct morphological 
reaches that are described in detail within Section 4.4.3 and depicted in Figure 4-86 (i.e., reaches SF1-
SF9). Habitat conditions vary within each reach but there are two primary reach types “transport 
reaches” and “response reaches”. The river channel alternates between more confined transport 
reaches with narrow valley widths and steep gradients (i.e., portions of reaches SF3, SF4, and SF9; Figure 
4-87) and less confined response reaches with higher sinuosity, lower gradients, and wider valley widths 
(i.e., portions of SF1, SF2, SF5, SF6, SF7, and SF8; Figure 4-88).  
Reach SF4, which is referred to as the canyon reach (Figure 4-89), has steep bedrock walls and a high 
gradient channel that forms waterfalls and rapids (Gendaszek et al. 2020). Under high flow conditions 
the canyon reach restricts passage of anadromous fish and delineates the upstream extent of spawning 
by most anadromous species except for summer steelhead and small numbers of Coho Salmon (Section

Channel Conditions 
Salmonid habitat requirements related to channel conditions include lateral and longitudinal complexity.  
With respect to lateral complexity, much of the Lower SFT River is naturally confined (Section 4.4.3.1). In 
areas where the gradient valley width is greater, the channel exhibits some sinuosity, braiding and 
meanders. There is no channel armoring or other physical structures related to Project operations that 
limit channel movement or floodplain connectivity. The Snohomish River Basin Salmonid Recovery 
Technical Committee (SRBSRTC 2002) rated the habitat condition of “shoreline condition and floodplain 
connectivity” of the Lower SFT as “intact”. 
The Lower SFT River exhibits a range of geomorphic units. The most common include step pools, boulder 
riffles, riffles, boulder runs, runs which vary in frequency between transport and response reaches. The 
proportions of geomorphic units in each reach are summarized within (Section 4.4.3.1). Overall, there 
appears to be significant heterogeneity in the longitudinal habitat complexity within the river segment 
and all salmonid life history stages are present including multi-year rearing juvenile steelhead.
